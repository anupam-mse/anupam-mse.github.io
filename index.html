01 · Questions

What pulls me in.

Not a list of samples. These are the questions I'd keep asking even if nobody paid me to.

The oxygen side wastes the most. Shrink the orange bar and green hydrogen gets cheaper. That is most of my PhD in one picture.

~0.3 Vlost on the oxygen side

Why is making oxygen so hard?

Four electrons, four protons and an O–O bond, all on one metal site. Every step has to go right. Nickel is cheap and almost good enough. I want to know what "almost" means at the atomic level.

secondsflash sintering time

What happens when a field hits a material?

An electric field can sinter ceramics in seconds instead of hours. A magnetic field can nudge spin and structure. Fields are a quiet knob in synthesis, and I like knobs nobody else turns.

operandomeasured while working

What is the catalyst, really?

The powder in the vial is rarely the thing doing the work. Under voltage it rebuilds itself. X-ray absorption and Raman let me watch that happen live, instead of guessing afterwards.

CO₂ · N₂next molecules

Can electricity make what chemistry plants make?

Fuels from CO₂ and ammonia from air, at room temperature, driven by renewable power. Water splitting is where I learned the tools. These are where I want to use them, with machine learning to search faster.

Nickel frameworks for water splitting

Nickel metal–organic frameworks where the only thing I change is the organic linker. Different linkers pull electrons differently, and the oxygen evolution rate follows. Then I use synchrotron X-rays at CHESS to watch each framework rebuild itself into the real catalyst under voltage.

05 · Off the bench

Why some notes agree.

Two notes sound sweet together when their frequencies form a simple ratio. Pick an interval, press play, and watch the shape the two waves draw.

Frequency ratio
3 : 2

The perfect fifth

Every third wave of one note lines up with every second wave of the other. The pattern closes quickly, so the ear hears it as calm. In Indian music it is Sa to Pa.

06 · Off the bench

The kitchen is a lab.

Cooking is chemistry you get to eat. A few reactions I think about at the stove.

140–165 °C

Why toast tastes like toast

The Maillard reaction. Sugars and amino acids react on hot, dry surfaces and make hundreds of flavour molecules. Water holds food near 100 °C, which is why boiled food never browns.

sugar + amino acid → melanoidins + aroma
pH > 8

Turmeric is a pH indicator

Curcumin is yellow in acid and turns red-brown in alkali. Spill haldi dal on soapy hands and watch it change. Same chemistry as my KOH electrolyte.

curcumin (yellow) + OH⁻ → phenolate (red)
~120 °C

Why the pressure cooker wins

Higher pressure raises the boiling point. A few extra degrees speeds softening a lot, so dal that needs an hour in a pot needs minutes under the whistle.

P ↑ → Tboil ↑ → rate ↑
tadka

Spices belong in hot oil

Many aroma compounds in cumin, mustard and chilli dissolve in fat, not water. Blooming them in hot ghee pulls the flavour out and carries it through the dish.

like dissolves like
chai

Milk softens bitter tea

Tea tannins bind to milk proteins like casein. Fewer free tannins reach your tongue, so strong chai tastes round instead of harsh.

tannin + casein → complex
−18 °C

Ice cream is a material

Ice crystals, air bubbles and fat droplets held in sugar syrup. Smaller crystals feel smoother, which is why fast freezing makes better ice cream.

foam + emulsion + crystals
